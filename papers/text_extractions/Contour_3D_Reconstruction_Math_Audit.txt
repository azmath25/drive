  Bottom line. The proposed anisotropy correction is a valid, strictly sharper1
  bound on the full spatial rim average. It is not currently a demonstrated im-
  provement over established 3D reconstruction methods, since practical images
  provide visible projected silhouettes rather than complete 3D rims, and the
  correction uses curvatures of the reconstructed shape. For image-accessible

cannot in general be recovered from visual-hull methods, even with many views [3, 4].
Modern multi-view surface methods combine photometric/differentiable losses and
representation-specific constraints [12, 13, 11]. There is no established reconstruc-
tion benchmark in the reviewed literature showing that enforcing (6) improves geo-
metric accuracy, Chamfer distance, silhouette IoU, inference speed, or robustness.
Such claims require experiments.

8.3 Evidence-based assessment
• Mathematical correctness: high. The incidence-coarea identity, original sharp
  theorem, anisotropy correction, projected formula, and convex special case have
  analytic proofs above.
• Theoretical interest: moderate. The correction improves the ellipse-perimeter
  estimate. Publication priority and substantial conceptual novelty remain unestab-
  lished.
• Direct 3D reconstruction value: currently low/unproven. The full rim is la-
  tent, the new deficit requires curvature data, and practical methods already use
  local curvature/surface regularization and image-fit constraints.
• Potential utility: real but conditional. Candidate-shape validation, noise-aware
  regularization experiments, and calibrated silhouettes for convex or nearly convex
  objects are plausible settings.
• Best research direction: empirical and geometric. Compare a baseline multi-
  view reconstruction with and without a well-defined contour-consistency penalty;
  include convex vs nonconvex families, hidden cavities, view subsampling, curva-
  ture noise, and topology mistakes. Separately investigate genus-specific optimal
  c(g) and stability.

  Novelty and priority flag. The curvature-ellipse identity and Willmore-type
